<Chapter n="01" title="The problem.">

DEIMR sells electronics, spare parts and device repairs. When I joined, pages took 9 to 15 seconds to load. On a marketplace, a slow page is a closed shop: people leave before they see a product.

The hard part wasn't only the code. The shop was live, and changing frameworks under a live shop is a big thing to ask for. I had to show management that React on its own wasn't the right fit, win the go-ahead to switch, and then do the move alone while customers kept using it.

</Chapter>

<Chapter n="02" title="The method.">
  <Steps>
    <Step title="Measure">Ran Lighthouse on the slowest routes to get a baseline, so every change had a number to beat.</Step>
    <Step title="Make the case">Showed management why React alone was holding the storefront back, and got the go-ahead to switch to Next.js.</Step>
    <Step title="Move a live shop, alone">Migrated the storefront to Next.js as the only engineer on it, while the platform stayed open. Next.js opened up server rendering and built-in caching.</Step>
    <Step title="Split by route">Loaded each route on demand with React.lazy and Suspense, so the first page stopped paying for every other page.</Step>
    <Step title="Shrink the images">Converted product imagery to WebP. On a marketplace, images are most of the weight.</Step>
    <Step title="Cache">Used Next.js caching so repeat visits and shared data stopped hitting the network every time.</Step>
  </Steps>
</Chapter>

<Chapter n="05" title="What I'd keep.">

Measure first. Lighthouse turned "it feels slow" into numbers the team could agree on, and every change after that had to move one.

The decision I'd defend: pushing for Next.js on a product that was already live. It was the harder conversation, and it's the reason the 1.5 seconds happened.
